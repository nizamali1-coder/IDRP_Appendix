Term Definition
Roles & Parties — who's doing what
Payer The insurance provider responsible for paying the bill — UnitedHealth/Optum, Anthem/Elevance, Aetna, Cigna, Humana, Blue Cross plans.Payers come in two flavors that determine which IDR portal applies: commercial (employer-sponsored plans, the focus of NSA) and government (Medicare/Medicaid, exempt from NSA). Within commercial, the deeper split is self-funded ERISA plans (always federal IDR) vs. fully-insured plans (state IDR in 22 bifurcated states, federal elsewhere). The first job in any IDR case is identifying which bucket the payer falls into — get this wrong and you lose the dispute on a procedural technicality, not on the merits.
Provider The clinician, group, or facility that rendered the service and is owed payment.NSA disputes are concentrated in hospital-based specialties — emergency medicine, anesthesia, radiology, pathology — because those clinicians often work out-of-network at facilities that ARE in-network with the patient's plan. The patient can't shop for an in-network anesthesiologist mid-surgery; that's exactly why NSA banned balance billing and routed the dispute to IDR. Per CMS PUF H1 2025: EM ~35%, Anesthesia ~21%, Radiology ~11%, Air Ambulance ~9% of federal IDR volume — the four specialties carry ~76% of all disputes.
Locum Tenens A temporary clinician hired to fill staffing gaps. Paid a flat per-diem by the staffing agency.The locum is financially decoupled from billing outcomes — they get paid whether the claim collects or not. The anesthesia group or hospital holds the billing exposure. Anesthesia groups use locums heavily (ASCs, weekends, vacation coverage), which is why anesthesia carries asymmetric IDR risk: the cost of providing the service is largely fixed (locum per-diem) but the recovery is entirely variable (whether the practice can navigate the IDR workflow). This is the structural reason a 21%-volume specialty consistently underfiles relative to its denial exposure.
Clearinghouse The intermediary that routes medical bills between provider billing systems and insurance providers. Top vendors: Waystar, Availity, Change Healthcare (Optum), Trizetto, Zelis.The clearinghouse is the layer that makes EHRs (Epic, athena, Tebra, eClinicalWorks) talk to dozens of payers without each provider building dozens of point-to-point integrations. Both directions of every transaction in the workflow flow through here: 837P claim out, 835 ERA back, 270/271 eligibility round-trip. Format errors get rejected at the clearinghouse before reaching the payer — which is why "claim denied" by a payer and "claim rejected" by a clearinghouse are different failure modes with different recovery paths. Most providers don't know which one happened until they look.
EHR / Practice Mgmt System EHR · PMS Where clinical documentation and billing data live for the provider. Epic, athena, Oracle Cerner, eClinicalWorks, Tebra (formerly Kareo), NextGen, AdvancedMD.The EHR is the source of truth for everything downstream: clinical notes, CPT/ICD-10 coding, time stamps, modifiers. The 837P claim file is generated from EHR data and pushed to the clearinghouse. EHR market split matters strategically — Epic dominates large hospital systems (gated by hospital IT, slow integrations), athena dominates ambulatory and independent practices (cloud-native, marketplace-driven, faster adoption). For a vendor selling to the OON-heavy independent ICP, athena is the integration target; Epic is the long road.
Billing Service / RCM Vendor RCM An outsourced revenue-cycle-management vendor that handles coding, submission, denials, and collections on behalf of a practice.RCM is segmented by what part of the workflow each vendor owns: Aspirion, Ventra, Zotec are full-stack national billers (~$30B+/yr in combined billings) — they do everything from charge capture to collections, but IDR is a small slice of their work and they're not specialized at it. HaloMD is IDR-only — pure-play, ~22% of federal IDR volume H1'25, ~50 staff, commission-only model (~20% of recovered amount). Pivotal Health is a VC-backed boutique — Bessemer-funded, claims 94% IDR win rate. Independents typically choose between full-stack at 10–20% contingency or IDR-only at 15–30%.
IDRE Indep. Dispute Resolution Entity The CMS-certified third-party arbitrator that picks the winning offer in an IDR case. MCMC, Medical Evaluators of Texas, Allmed, others.IDREs are not vendor-selectable — CMS assigns from a certified pool. Both parties have limited rights to challenge an assigned IDRE for conflict of interest, but in practice you get who you get. The IDRE's job is binary: pick provider's offer OR pick payer's offer (baseball arbitration — never a number in between). Their decision must be issued within 30 business days; payment within 30 days of decision. The losing party pays the IDRE's fee ($200–$840 single dispute, $268–$1,173 batched) — but the $115 admin fee per party is non-refundable regardless of outcome. Even if you win, you're net $115 down on entry costs.
CMS Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services The federal agency that administers Medicare/Medicaid and operates the federal IDR portal at nsa-idr.cms.gov.CMS's role in NSA/IDR is dual: regulator (writes the rules under 45 CFR §149.510, certifies IDREs, sets fee schedules) and operator (runs the federal IDR portal, publishes the PUF dataset). The portal is the only filing path for federal disputes — there is no API, no automation, no batch upload for non-conforming cases. Every dispute is filed through a web interface, claim by claim, with a 4-business-day window after open negotiation expires. CMS also handles complaints (1-800-985-3059, nsahelpdesk@cms.hhs.gov) but ASA tracks ~7% of complaints translate to formal violation findings — the enforcement bar is high.
Data Standards & Files — the formats that flow between parties
837P HIPAA EDI The HIPAA-standard electronic file format for a Professional (physician) claim. Submitted from provider → clearinghouse → payer.The "P" distinguishes it from 837I (Institutional/facility) and 837D (Dental). Every NSA-eligible dispute starts as an 837P. The file carries the CPT codes, ICD-10 codes, modifiers, NPIs (billing and rendering), patient demographics, and service dates — everything the payer needs to adjudicate. If anything is missing or malformed, the clearinghouse rejects the file before the payer sees it. This is "rejection" not "denial" — different failure mode, different recovery: rejection means fix and resubmit; denial means dispute or appeal.
835 ERA Electronic Remittance Advice Electronic Remittance Advice — the payer's response file showing what was paid, what was denied, and which denial codes apply.The 835 is the most information-dense file in the entire workflow — it carries the QPA paid, the CARC/RARC denial codes, the patient responsibility amount, contractual adjustments, and remarks. It's what triggers Phase 2 (denial management) in the workflow. Routes back through the same clearinghouse as the 837P went out (Waystar, Availity, Change Healthcare). Reading an 835 well is the single highest-leverage skill in RCM — it's where you find out which denials are appealable, which are IDR-eligible, and which need to be written off. Aspirion, Ventra, Zotec, HaloMD all build their core competency around 835 interpretation.
270 / 271 Eligibility transactions Eligibility transactions. 270 = the inquiry sent by the provider ("is this patient covered?"). 271 = the payer's response.Runs in real-time through the same clearinghouse layer as the 837P. Eligibility is the first defensible line against avoidable denials downstream — if the 271 says "patient not covered" or "different plan," the practice can stop work, escalate the patient to billing, or re-verify before the encounter. Stale or skipped eligibility checks are a top driver of "patient not covered" denials weeks later, when recovery is much harder. For OON anesthesia/EM, eligibility also flags whether the encounter falls under NSA (in-network facility, OON physician) — which is the trigger for the entire IDR pathway.
CARC / RARC Adjustment / Remark codes Claim Adjustment Reason Codes / Remittance Advice Remark Codes — the alphanumeric codes inside an 835 that tell you why a claim was denied or short-paid.CARC = the reason category (CO-50 "non-covered service," CO-29 "time limit expired," etc.); RARC = the supplementary remark explaining context. There are hundreds of combinations and no industry-standard taxonomy — each payer uses CARCs slightly differently, and the same CARC from UHC vs. Anthem can mean different things in practice. Interpreting them correctly determines whether a denial is appealable, IDR-eligible, or a write-off. This is exactly the kind of pattern-recognition-over-time work where AI applied to historical 835 data is a real moat — payer-specific behavior learned from feedback loops at scale.
CPT Current Procedural Terminology Current Procedural Terminology — the AMA-maintained code set that identifies what service was performed (e.g., 99285 = high-complexity ED visit).Pairs with ICD-10 on every claim — both are required. CPT alone won't pass; the clearinghouse rejects incomplete claims at the format check. CPT errors and CPT/ICD-10 mismatches are different failure modes: format errors are caught at the clearinghouse before reaching the payer; medical-necessity mismatches are caught at adjudication and denied. Coders translate clinical documentation into CPT codes — for anesthesia, this includes time units, base units, and physical-status modifiers. Coders are the single most expensive RCM line item, which is why outsourcing to Aspirion, Ventra, Zotec is the dominant pattern for mid-size practices.
ICD-10 Diagnosis codes International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision — the diagnosis code set (e.g., I21.9 = acute myocardial infarction).Required alongside CPT on every claim — both are mandatory. CPT says what was done; ICD-10 says why it was medically necessary. If the diagnosis doesn't support the procedure, the claim denies at adjudication with a CARC like "service not medically necessary." NSA/IDR disputes are driven by CPT (procedures and the rates paid for them), not ICD-10 — but you can't even submit the claim to fight over without ICD-10 attached. The ICD-10/CPT pair is the single most consequential coding decision in the whole workflow, which is why coding errors are the #1 source of preventable denials.
Modifier A 2-character suffix on a CPT code that conveys context — physical-status modifier for anesthesia, bilateral procedure, telehealth, etc.Modifiers are how the same CPT code conveys different operational realities — for anesthesia in particular, the physical-status modifier (P1 healthy, P5 moribund) directly affects payment because anesthesia billing uses base units × physical status × time units. Wrong or missing modifiers are a top cause of denials, and the rules vary by payer: UHC accepts modifier combinations that Anthem rejects, BCBS plans differ state by state. No public API exists for payer-specific modifier rules — the knowledge is locked in coders' heads or in proprietary RCM rule engines. This is exactly the kind of payer-behavior-pattern problem where AI trained on ERA feedback loops creates a competitive moat over time.
EDI Electronic Data Interchange Electronic Data Interchange — the umbrella protocol family that 837/835/270/271 all use.Mostly invisible to end users; lives at the clearinghouse layer. EDI is HIPAA-mandated for all standard healthcare transactions, which is why Waystar, Availity, Change Healthcare, Trizetto, Zelis exist as a layer at all — they handle the EDI translation between practice management systems (which speak proprietary formats) and payer systems (which require strict EDI). For a vendor building IDR-adjacent products, the practical implication is that you don't talk EDI directly — you integrate with the clearinghouse via REST API and let it handle the EDI plumbing. Stedi is the modern API-first option that wraps EDI in JSON.
HL7 / FHIR Clinical data standards Standards for moving patient records between EHRs and apps. HL7 v2 is the legacy messaging format (still dominant). FHIR is the modern REST/JSON-based standard.Important to understand what HL7/FHIR is not for the IDR economy: billing transactions don't flow through FHIR — they flow through EDI (837P, 835, 270/271). FHIR is for clinical data: patient demographics, allergies, lab results, clinical notes. So when someone says "we integrate with the EHR via FHIR," that gets you clinical context (useful for the IDR offer letter's 7-factor argument) but it does NOT get you the claim file or the ERA. For IDR-relevant work, you need both the FHIR layer (clinical context) AND the EDI/clearinghouse layer (financial transactions). They're separate stacks.
NPI National Provider Identifier National Provider Identifier — the 10-digit unique ID for every billing provider in the US. Required on every claim.Two NPIs appear on every 837P: the billing NPI (which entity gets paid — usually the practice or group) and the rendering NPI (the individual clinician who performed the service). The distinction matters for IDR batching: one of the five batching rules is same billing NPI — multi-location groups that bill under different entity NPIs can't batch claims across sites. NPIs also appear in the CMS PUF (which lets you attribute IDR volume to specific operators — that's how we know HaloMD is ~22% of federal volume) and in PitchBook (which lets you attribute NPIs to PE owners — that's how we know 88% of federal IDR is PE-affiliated).
Concepts & Regulatory Terms — the rules and constructs
NSA No Surprises Act The No Surprises Act — federal law (effective Jan 2022) that bans balance-billing patients for emergency or OON care at in-network facilities, and creates the IDR process for resolving payment disputes.NSA fundamentally changed the OON economy. Before NSA: OON providers could balance-bill the patient for the gap between billed charges and what insurance paid. After NSA: the patient is held harmless at the in-network rate — the dispute is between the provider and the payer, mediated by IDR. This shifted billions of dollars in disputed claims out of patient collections (where it generated political backlash) and into a structured arbitration process. The provider/payer dispute volume is the entire reason this market exists. NSA covers commercial plans only; Medicare/Medicaid have separate rules.
IDR Independent Dispute Resolution Independent Dispute Resolution — the NSA-mandated arbitration process. After failed direct negotiation, both parties submit best-and-final offers; an IDRE picks one.Federal IDR runs through the CMS portal at nsa-idr.cms.gov; 22 states run their own state IDR processes for fully-insured plans (TX, NY, CA, FL, GA, etc.). The first decision in any IDR case is routing — federal vs. state — and getting it wrong means permanent forfeit. Once routed, the workflow is: 30-day open negotiation → 4-business-day window to initiate → submit offer letter addressing 7 statutory factors with 5 data sources → IDRE selects one offer → payment within 30 days. Every step has a non-appealable deadline. H1 2025: 1.46M federal disputes, ~$11B in disputed claim volume.
QPA Qualifying Payment Amount Qualifying Payment Amount — the median in-network contracted rate as of January 31, 2019, indexed annually for CPI-U inflation.The QPA is what the payer pays initially on an OON claim — and the floor every IDR offer letter has to argue against. The 2019 anchor is permanent; only inflation moves the number, and CPI-U has lagged healthcare wage and cost growth by several percentage points per year since 2019. This gap between "what the QPA says the service is worth" and "what it actually costs to deliver in 2026" is what generates the IDR dispute volume in the first place. Empirical confirmation: median provider-favorable IDR awards land at ~4.59× QPA per CMS PUF — when arbitrators look at the same dispute and weigh the 7 statutory factors, they routinely conclude the QPA is several multiples below appropriate. See §C.1 Stage 05 for full mechanics including the IRS-published inflation multipliers.
OON Out-of-Network Out-of-Network — the provider does not have a contract with the patient's insurance plan.NSA disputes apply only to OON claims at in-network facilities (or emergency situations). This is the structural reason hospital-based specialists are concentrated in the IDR pool: an emergency physician can be OON with a payer while working at a hospital that IS in-network with that payer — the patient has no realistic way to know in advance, which is exactly the "surprise" the NSA was designed to address. For elective care, NSA applicability requires either an emergency situation or that the patient lacked meaningful choice (e.g., anesthesia for a scheduled surgery — you don't pick your anesthesiologist). The OON test combined with the in-network-facility test is what gates a claim into NSA jurisdiction at all.
ERISA Employee Retirement Income Security Act The federal law governing employer-sponsored benefit plans, enacted in 1974. ERISA created federal protection for employee benefits — and, critically, federal preemption of state law. Most large employers (Fortune 500, ~60% of US commercial enrollees) self-fund under ERISA — paying claims directly from operating cash rather than buying insurance.ERISA is the reason the bifurcated state/federal IDR system exists at all. The mechanism is preemption: ERISA explicitly preempts state laws that "relate to" employee benefit plans, which means state insurance regulators have no jurisdiction over self-funded employer plans. The employer bears the financial risk directly and pays claims from operating cash, using a third-party administrator like UnitedHealthcare or Cigna as the claims processor and network rental. Stop-loss insurance covers catastrophic risk. Why employers do this: ERISA preemption exempts them from state-level benefit mandates, state premium taxes, and state-level utilization review rules — yielding a meaningful cost advantage over fully-insured plans. The remaining ~40% of commercial enrollees are in fully-insured plans (employer buys insurance from a carrier who bears the risk and is regulated as an insurer at the state level).

For NSA/IDR, this split is the entire reason the federal IDR pathway exists. Without it, there would be no regulatory forum for disputes on the ~60% of claims that flow through ERISA plans, since states can't regulate them. All ERISA self-funded plans route to federal IDR regardless of which state the patient or provider is in; fully-insured plans route to state IDR in the 22 bifurcated states, federal elsewhere. The operational hard problem: you cannot tell whether a plan is self-funded from the insurance card alone — the patient experience is identical (UHC card, UHC network, UHC member services), but the regulatory regime underneath is fundamentally different. Self-funded status requires plan documents or payer verification. This is why misrouting is the #1 procedural forfeit in federal IDR data — and the basis for HaloMD's four BCBS lawsuits, where the contested question is whether plans HaloMD treated as ERISA were actually self-funded.
Bifurcated State A state with its own IDR process for fully-insured plans, while ERISA self-funded plans still go to federal IDR. 22 states are bifurcated as of 2026 (TX, NY, CA, FL, GA, etc.).Bifurcated states are the source of most procedural forfeits: every dispute requires a fresh routing decision (federal portal vs. state portal), and each state's portal has its own rules, timelines, fee structures, and forms. Texas alone has six different IDR pathways depending on plan type and service. There is no unified state IDR API; each state portal is a separate manual integration. This is exactly the kind of regulatory variability that defeats general-purpose RCM tools and why no vendor has built scaled state-IDR automation. State filing rates run 2–5% (vs. ~9% federal) — most of the state pool is structurally unfiled, which is also why no primary-source state IDR outcome data exists at the level CMS PUF provides federally.
Baseball Arbitration The NSA's IDR design: each side submits one offer; the IDRE picks one or the other — never a number in between.Baseball arbitration is the single most important strategic constraint in IDR. Forces both parties to submit reasonable offers — extreme positions lose because the IDRE will simply pick the other side's number. This is the opposite of "splitting the difference" arbitration: there is no compromise outcome, only winner and loser. The provider's offer letter is the entire game — it must be high enough to recover meaningful money but defensible against the 7 statutory factors. The asymmetry: payers have armies of analysts and proprietary QPA databases; independent providers have one biller and a Word template. This is why HaloMD-class specialists exist and why their 22% volume share is a moat, not a coincidence.
Encounters / Cases The unit-of-work conversion between clinical care and IDR. An encounter is one clinical visit (FHIR Encounter resource); a case is the IDR-specific dispute object — a single filing that may bundle multiple claims from one or many encounters.The terminology shifts at every layer of the stack. Practices think in encounters (patient visits in the EHR); billers think in claims (one 837P per CPT-line bundle from each encounter); IDR specialists think in cases (one dispute object that batches up to 25 claims under the 5 batching rules). Each layer drops information about the next: encounter-level systems don't know which claims are OON; claim-level systems don't know which can batch into the same case; only specialty tools (HaloMD-class) carry the full unit-of-work conversion through to filing. Concrete math: a multi-physician anesthesia group running 100+ encounters per week generates 200–400 CPT-line claims; only the OON-denied subset is IDR-eligible; that pool then collapses into perhaps 8–15 batched cases at the filing layer. A HaloMD portal screenshot showing "330 Total Submitted CPTs" at one practice represents roughly 80–110 underlying encounters and 30–40 IDR cases — the conversion factor that gets lost without specialty tooling. See §C Batching for the rules that drive the claim-to-case collapse.
Batching Filing multiple disputes as a single IDR case to share fees and offer-letter work.Batching is the only way to make IDR economically viable for small-dollar claims. Without it, the $115 admin fee + $200–$840 IDRE fee make individual filings unprofitable for any dispute under ~$1,500. With batching, one fee + one offer letter covers up to 25 claims — per-claim cost drops 90%+. But the rules are punishing: same payer, same plan type, same billing NPI, same CPT family, 30-day service-date window. One non-conforming claim voids the entire batch. The 4-business-day initiation window applies to the batch as a whole. This is exactly the kind of combinatorial filtering problem where automation creates real value — finding the optimal batch grouping across thousands of pending denials is the difference between filing 20% of eligible volume and filing 80%.
PUF Public Use File Public Use File — the de-identified dataset CMS publishes for federal IDR cases. H1 2025 PUF: ~1.46M dispute records.The only primary-source dataset on IDR outcomes — every other figure in the readout is derived from vendor reports or methodologically estimated. The PUF includes initiating party (NPI level), payer, dispute amount, IDRE selected, winner, and award amount. This is what makes claims like "HaloMD = 22% of federal volume" or "median provider-favorable award is 4.59× QPA" defensible — they're computed directly from PUF rows. The PUF's structural limitation is what it doesn't cover: state IDR outcomes (no equivalent dataset exists), pre-IDR settlements (most negotiations resolve before filing), and provider economics (the PUF shows awards but not contingency rates or net economics to the provider). For state-level analysis or unit economics, you have to derive.
Capitation A payment model where the payer pays the provider a flat monthly fee per patient regardless of services rendered.Common in primary care and Medicare Advantage; mostly absent from emergency, anesthesia, and radiology because those services aren't predictable per-patient. Listed here because someone reading about fee-for-service IDR disputes should understand what the alternative model is and why it doesn't show up in this market. Capitation effectively eliminates the fee-for-service-rate dispute (because there are no per-service rates to dispute) but introduces different dynamics: actuarial risk shifts to the provider, and the disputes become about panel size, attribution, and quality bonuses rather than CPT-level rates. NSA/IDR is fundamentally a fee-for-service problem.
Risk-Sharing Payment arrangements where providers share financial upside or downside with payers based on outcomes, cost targets, or quality metrics. Includes ACOs, bundled payments, value-based care contracts.Largely separate from the NSA/IDR economy, which lives in fee-for-service OON. Risk-sharing arrangements typically apply to in-network care managed by ACOs or value-based care contracts — by design, they reduce the per-claim disputes that feed IDR by tying payment to outcomes rather than units of service. Listed here because the deck audience may ask why IDR matters when the industry is "moving to value-based care" — the answer is that hospital-based specialties (EM, anesthesia, radiology, pathology) are structurally hard to put in risk-sharing models because the demand is unpredictable and the services are episodic. Those specialties remain fee-for-service, which is why they remain the IDR-dispute concentration.

Methodology Note

Five rules govern: same payer (UHC ✓), same plan type (commercial ERISA ✓), same billing NPI (the practice ✓), same CPT family (29826 and adjacent arthroscopy codes 29827–29828 group together ✓), and a rolling 30-day service-date window. Eric's case clusters cleanly with 11 other shoulder-arthroscopy disputes from the same period; the remaining 35 split into 4 separate batches by payer or service line.

The offer letter is not written from scratch. Stage 05 Deep-Dive · QPA Mechanics

The QPA is frozen at 2019, indexed forward by CPI-U.

The Qualifying Payment Amount is defined under 45 CFR §149.140 as the median in-network contracted rate as of January 31, 2019, for the same/similar service, by the same/similar specialty, in the same geographic region. The 2019 base is permanently fixed. Only inflation adjustment moves the number — the federal regulation specifies CPI-U (Consumer Price Index for All Urban Consumers).

QPA2024 = Median 2019 contracted rate × cumulative CPI-U multiplier (~1.21× as of 2024 per IRS Notice 2024-01)

Worked example from IRS guidance: A service with a $1,500 median 2019 rate becomes $1,813 in 2024. Same CPT code, same payer, same geography — locked to the 2019 anchor with general inflation on top.

Why this matters for the deck's argument: Healthcare wages, locum rates, and malpractice premiums have risen faster than general CPI-U since 2019. The QPA structurally undercounts true 2026 cost. Empirical confirmation: median provider-favorable IDR awards land at ~4.59× QPA in CMS PUF data — meaning when arbitrators look at the same dispute and weigh the 7 statutory factors, they routinely conclude the QPA is several multiples below appropriate. The QPA is not "the right rate." It's the floor that drives the dispute volume.

§149.510(c) — the five batching gates that make IDR economically viable.

Single-claim IDR filing costs $315–$955 per dispute (IDRE arbitration fee + $115 admin fee per party). For a typical $700 anesthesia underpayment, the fees eat the recovery — most small-dollar claims are uneconomic to file individually. The Act's drafters anticipated this and built batched determinations into 45 CFR §149.510(c): one dispute filing can cover multiple claims, dropping per-claim cost from $315–955 to ~$30. Every claim in a batch must clear all five gates below.

Why batching is the entire wedge

The $119–215B unfiled gap (Appendix §B.1) isn't a market failure — it's a batching gap. ~40–50% of unfiled claims fall below the single-claim hurdle. At batched per-claim cost, those same claims become economically filable. Anesthesia is the canonical case: per-procedure repetitive billing × same CPT × same payer × small geographic spread = a textbook §149.510(c) cohort. Gate Requirement What the rule says Why a batch fails this gate
1§149.510(c)(3)(i) Same payer / same plan All claims in the batch must involve the same group health plan or health insurance issuer, and the same coverage offered under that plan. The IDR entity must receive a single set of plan-level information per dispute. Mixing UnitedHealthcare and Aetna in one batch. Mixing UHC fully-insured commercial with UHC self-funded ERISA (different plans even if same payer entity). Plan year boundary crossed mid-batch.
2§149.510(c)(3)(ii) Same QPA service code family Claims must be for items or services that furnished under the same or similar billing service code. CMS guidance interprets this as the same CPT/HCPCS code OR codes that share a Qualifying Payment Amount (QPA) under the same service category and modifier set. Mixing CPT 00790 (upper-abdominal anesthesia) with CPT 99284 (ER visit) in one batch. Mixing time-based anesthesia codes with flat-rate procedural codes. The QPA family rule is the most-litigated batching test.
3§149.510(c)(3)(iii) 30-day furnishing window All items/services in the batch must have been furnished within a 30-day period. The clock starts on the earliest date of service in the batch and runs forward 30 calendar days. The 30-day window references the date of service, not the billing date, denial date, or open negotiation start. Including a claim with a service date 35 days after the earliest claim in the batch. Re-using claims from a prior expired batch without re-anchoring the 30-day window. Aged-out claims must file as solo disputes.
4§149.510(c)(3)(iv) Same provider or facility All claims must be from the same provider, group of providers, or facility. CMS interprets this as the same billing NPI (Type 1 individual or Type 2 organizational) and the same Tax ID (TIN). A group practice billing under one organizational NPI is one provider for this purpose. Mixing claims from two different anesthesia groups even if they cover the same hospital. Mixing solo-NPI billings from an associate physician with group-NPI billings from the same practice. NPI changes mid-batch (rare but blocking).
5§149.510(c)(1) Open negotiation period completed The 30-day open negotiation period must have run out before IDR initiation. The Notice of Open Negotiation kicks off the 30-day clock, and the IDR window opens only after the 30 days expire. For a batch, the open-negotiation requirement applies to each underlying claim (or to the batch-level negotiation, where the payer has agreed to negotiate the batch as a single matter). Including a fresh claim where the open negotiation hasn't expired. Mixing claims with different open-negotiation start dates such that the latest one hasn't matured. This is the most operationally tracked gate — F3 IDR Forecaster maintains a per-claim clock.
The unit economics — single claim vs. batched

SINGLE CLAIM: $115 (admin) × 2 parties + $200–$840 (IDRE arbitration) = $430–$1,070 per dispute · loser pays the IDRE fee, so winning provider absorbs $115; losing pays the full IDRE fee.
BATCHED (e.g., 25 claims): Same fee structure applies once per batch · per-claim cost: $17–$43 · ~95% reduction.
Result: a $700 underpayment that's uneconomic to file solo becomes a no-brainer batched. The unfiled gap collapses where batching is mechanically feasible.

The 7-factor Notice of Offer — building the legal case for your payment proposal.

IDR is baseball-style arbitration: the IDR entity selects exactly one of the two offers (provider's vs. payer's), no compromise, no splitting the difference. The Notice of Offer is your case. A strong underlying claim with a weak Notice loses to a weaker claim with a stronger Notice. The offer letter is the legal artifact that wins or loses the dispute — not the underlying clinical record.

Why offer-letter quality decides everything

Under 45 CFR §149.510(c)(4)(iii), the IDR entity is required to consider seven specific factors when selecting between offers. The Notice of Offer must address each factor with credible supporting information. Provider-favorable awards land at ~4.59× QPA on average (Georgetown CHIR 2024) — but only when the Notice presents all seven factors with documented evidence. Offers that submit a number without supporting the seven factors lose ~67% of the time even when they're objectively correct on the underlying claim. Prohibited references in the Notice of Offer

The IDR entity is statutorily prohibited from considering certain rate references under 45 CFR §149.510(c)(4)(iv). Including these in your Notice of Offer doesn't help — and signals an unsophisticated submission. Do not reference: (1) Medicare reimbursement rates; (2) Medicaid reimbursement rates; (3) CHIP rates; (4) TRICARE rates; (5) "usual and customary charges" or any billed-charge benchmark; (6) the provider's billed amount as evidence of fair value. The QPA, FAIR Health, market data, and the seven §149.510(c)(4)(iii) factors are the permitted universe.

The problem

You can't manage what you can't qualify into a batch.

Most independent practices have no visibility into how much they're being underpaid on out-of-network claims — and even less visibility into the pattern of underpayment. The data lives in athenaOne (paid amounts, CPT codes, geography), in CMS public files (75th-percentile precedent for similar disputes), and in FAIR Health benchmarks (80th-percentile cross-check) — but reconciling those three sources takes RCM expertise the practice doesn't have in-house.

The federal No Surprises Act IDR process exists to recover the difference, but it only works at scale through batched filing under §149.510(c) — same payer, same CPT family, same NPI, same 30-day window, plus code parity. Those five dimensions aren't just a filing rule. They're the shape of cohort-level underpayment: when one payer systematically pays below market on one CPT family across many patients, that's not bad luck — that's a pattern, and §149.510(c) is the legal mechanism designed to surface it.

Without quantifying that opportunity first, practices either skip IDR entirely (leaving 6-figure underpayments on the table every year) or pay an outside RCM firm 25–35% of recovered cash to chase claims they couldn't size themselves. What CMS actually mandates for IDR filing

NOA Web Form fields · 45 CFR §149.510 statutory factors · what's required vs. what's argument

Filing portal nsa-idr.cms.gov
Bottom line. CMS mandates very few fields on the Notice of Offer web form — the disputing party submits a payment offer, declares practice size and geographic region, and uploads supporting evidence as PDF attachments. The arbitrator (certified IDR entity) makes a baseball-arbitration choice between the two parties' offers. Every supporting argument beyond the QPA itself is optional evidence, not a required submission field.

Procedural sequence · 75-business-day clock Submission timeline

  1. Open Negotiation Period — 30 business days starting from the day after the initial payment or notice of denial. Provider sends Open Negotiation Notice to payer (DOL Form OMB 1210-0169). Either party can initiate IDR if no agreement reached.
  2. Notice of IDR Initiation — submitted within 4 business days after open negotiation ends, via nsa-idr.cms.gov. Initiating party identifies items/services, provides claim details, and selects preferred Certified IDR Entity (IDRE).
  3. IDRE Selection Response — non-initiating party has 3 business days to agree or propose alternate IDRE. If silent, initiating party's preferred IDRE is selected.
  4. Eligibility determination — IDRE confirms eligibility within 5 business days. If ineligible, dispute is closed; if eligible, both parties enter offer-submission phase.
  5. Notice of Offer submission — both parties have 10 business days to submit final payment offer + supporting evidence (PDF attachments). This is where the offer letter goes.
  6. IDRE determination — arbitrator selects one of the two offers as final. Decision rendered within 30 business days of IDRE selection. Payment due within 30 calendar days of determination.

Optional PDF attachments · 45 CFR §149.510(c)(4) Supporting evidence (none mandatory)

The IDRE arbitrator may consider these factors in addition to the QPA when selecting between offers. They are optional submissions; the disputing party chooses what to attach and argue. The strongest arguments are typically: prior contracted rate, patient acuity, and provider credentials — in that order.

Practical reality. The statute lists seven factors the IDRE "may consider" beyond the QPA. None of these are required submissions — the arbitrator can reach a decision on QPA alone. In real-world filings, three factors do most of the heavy lifting: prior contracted rate (F), patient acuity (C), and provider training/quality (A). The remaining four are sometimes argued but rarely move the determination.

Skip it for typical mid-market anesthesia groups.

Payer market share is technically allowable evidence under §149.510(c)(4)(iii)(B), but it is rarely the deciding factor in IDR awards. The arbitrator weighs the QPA against prior contracted rates, FAIR Health benchmarks, CMS PUF precedent, and patient acuity — market share is a footnote argument that reinforces "payer leverage depressed the QPA," but doesn't typically move the award materially.

How Folio3 derives the CMS PUF 75th-percentile

The PUF doesn't publish "the 75th-percentile" as a column — Folio3 computes it by matching the dispute we're sizing against historically similar closed disputes.
DERIVATION

For a given claim or candidate cohort, Folio3 selects matching PUF rows on:

match = same CPT family ∧ similar payer ∧ similar state/region ∧ similar provider specialty
window = rolling 6 quarters of PUF data

Then computes the 75th-percentile of prevailing offer amount across the matched dispute set:

benchmark_cms_75 = percentile(matched_disputes.prevailing_offer, 75)
Interpretation: in disputes that look like ours, the winning offer cleared at or above this number 25% of the time. It's the high-watermark precedent we'd anchor an offer near — not a hard floor, but where IDR entities have been awarding.
FAIR HEALTH 80th · HOW IT'S SOURCED

FAIR Health publishes percentiles directly — we don't compute them, we lookup:

benchmark_fh_80 = FH.lookup(cpt_code, geozip, percentile=80)
FAIR Health is a charge-distribution dataset — it tells us what providers in the region charge, not what payers pay. The 80th-percentile is a long-standing benchmark used in pre-NSA Usual & Customary determinations. We surface it as a charge-context cross-check, not a settlement target.

What "adjacent" means — and what it does not mean.

The F3 IDR Forecaster is not a generic underpayment tool. It's a specialty mechanism that triggers on a specific legal pathway. Adjacency has to be defined against that wedge, not against RCM broadly.

The Forecaster reads athena Charge data, identifies systematic out-of-network underpayment patterns over the trailing 12 months, qualifies eligible claims for batched IDR filing under 45 CFR §149.510(c), and drafts the offer letter. Recovery without an RCM firm. Without a commission split.

That positions the Forecaster between two existing layers in the athena Marketplace — neither of which it competes with directly, but both of which inform what providers will pay for what:

Layer above · in-network underpayment

Contract variance & recovery vendors

Tools like Rivet, MD Clarity, SlicedHealth, and Adonis compare actual remittances against contracted rates. Underpayments are appealed through standard payer channels. This is the adjacency that matters most for pricing comparison.

Layer below · IDR specialists

Pure-play IDR services

Outside the athena Marketplace: HaloMD (~22% of federal IDR volume, ~20% commission) and similar pure-play firms file disputes for OON claims as a service, taking a cut of recovery. This is the labor F3 displaces — not on the marketplace, but the substitute.

Why this distinction matters for pricing

The variance vendors charge SaaS subscriptions for visibility into in-network leakage that providers can already chase. The IDR specialists charge a 15–25% commission on disputed dollars they recover under NSA. The Forecaster's wedge is the SaaS structure of the first layer applied to the legal mechanism of the second. That positioning sets a floor (above generic variance tools — more domain) and a ceiling (well below 20% commission — different cost structure).

§M1 · ModMed-specific gotchas Patterns Folio3 must accommodate that don't exist on athenaOne.

Two API surfaces, one auth, one mental model

ModMed exposes three API surfaces — Certified FHIR (read-only, ONC-certified), Proprietary FHIR (read/write, FHIR-shaped), and EMA Proprietary REST (charges, payments, bills, financials). Folio3 uses the latter two. The Certified FHIR API does not carry billing data. Charges/financials live on the EMA Proprietary REST surface only. Auth is OAuth2 + per-tenant scoped tokens; one set of credentials covers both surfaces but each call targets its own base URL.

Coverage payload shape changed in 2024

Per ModMed release notes, when Coverage CREATE was added, the Coverage READ / SEARCH payload structure changed. Pin to a version in your client library and verify each tenant is on the matching schema; a mismatched payload will silently drop subscriber-relationship and plan-class fields that gate the federal-vs-state routing decision.

Charges may not always be tied to an Encounter

From ModMed's docs: practices using MMPM as their PM may receive inbound charges from other applications that aren't associated with an Encounter. Folio3's encounter-anchored cohort logic must tolerate this — for unmoored charges, fall back to (Patient × DOS × CPT × NPI) as the cohort key rather than (Encounter × CPT × NPI). Same dispute under §149.510(c) batching rules either way.

Billing-provider NPI must map to a MMPM Business Unit

ModMed's PM segments providers into "Business Units." If your application processes charges, you'll often need the provider's NPI to map the charge to the Billing Provider's Business Unit in MMPM — Folio3 derives practice size per CMS Notice of Offer tier from this mapping (count of active billing-provider NPIs in the tenant's Business Units), the same way athenaOne uses PractitionerRole count.

Auto-bill-from-charge is a per-customer config flag

Some tenants auto-create Bills from Charges; some don't. Your charge-to-bill join logic must handle both. If the practice doesn't auto-create, the charge-side gap signature is computed before any Bill exists — this is fine for Phase 1 cohort assembly, since the cohort is built from charges (billed vs allowed) not bills. For closure analytics in Phase 2, the email-mailbox parser + Posting matching does the reconciliation regardless of which side bill creation flowed from.

Sandbox is generic — tenant-specific quirks emerge in production

ModMed sandboxes are generic databases with sample Patients, Practitioners, Locations, and Encounters. Specialty-vertical fields (e.g., EMA Pain Management vs. EMA Plastic Surgery vs. EMA Ophthalmology specialty modules) don't fully appear in the generic sandbox. Plan for a 2-week production-tenant validation phase per specialty before declaring an integration "done."
